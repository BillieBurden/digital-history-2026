The dataset was compiled by the Washington Post and then published on GitHub through one of their investigative data reporters, Andrew Ba Tran. The data comes from the U.S. Department of the Interior’s (DOI) Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative Investigative Report Vol. II and the Washington Post’s year-long investigation of the boarding schools. The DOI’s research goals were to investigate “the loss of human life and lasting consequences of the Federal Indian boarding school system” and to “assist in the effort to recover the histories of these institutions” (Newland 9). Whereas the Post created this file and others to “facilitate additional research” (Hedgpeth).

Each row is a record for one Indian boarding school. The columns then list the details for that school: its name and state, its years of operation, the specific Tribes that attended, and any other names the school used. There are also counts for the deceased people and burial sites found by both the DOI and the Washington Post, with notes on whether those graves were onsite (at the school) or off-site (in another cemetery). Finally, each row has the school's exact latitude and longitude coordinates and a note on whether the location was verified.

There are many limitations to this dataset and the overall research. The DOI report acknowledges that its count of 973 deaths is incomplete because many federal records were destroyed, lost, or never created. More confinements come from the date range of their research. Victims who do not fall between the years of 1819-1969 could get omitted from the data, ultimately narrowing the qualifications for who gets counted and who doesn’t. Additionally, The Washington Post noted that school administrators, such as Richard Henry Pratt at Carlisle, often sent terminally ill children home so their deaths would occur off-campus and not appear on official school registers (Hedgpeth). With a broader view that could show that the perpetrators controlled the validity of the data, leaving children unaccounted for. Another issue arises due to the DOI withholding the names of the children they identified, making it impossible to verify how much their list overlaps with the Post’s larger count. This creates a gap in our ability to cross-reference specific identities. Additionally, the dataset relies on a Western geospatial grid that cannot easily account for lost cemeteries/burial sites. For example, the Post used historical maps to find burial sites that have since been paved over or turned into farmland, such as the soybean field at the Genoa Indian Industrial School.

FINAL PROJECT

The biggest finding from this project is that the federal Indian boarding school system never ended. About 84 schools are still in operation as of 2024, though now, they are mostly run by tribes themselves under federal funding with goals of maintaining and encouraging Native culture.
I came into this final project from Milestone 3, where my hypothesis was that the federal Indian boarding school system expanded unevenly across the U.S., with the Southwest and Plains states bearing the heaviest share, and that new school openings surged after the Peace Policy in 1869 before declining after the Indian Reorganization Act in 1934. To explore this, I worked with a Washington Post dataset of 418 federal boarding schools across 37 states. I had cleaned this dataset in Milestone 1, mostly by dropping unhelpful columns, and then had to clean it again in Milestone 3 to actually parse out useful information, like splitting the operation_dates column into separate open and close years. From there, I built a bar chart of schools by state and a line chart of openings per year. Both charts confirmed parts of my hypothesis. Oklahoma, Arizona, and New Mexico clearly had the highest school counts, and the line chart showed increases after 1869. But like I noted in milestone 3, the line chart also showed something I wasn't expecting. Right after the Indian Reorganization Act in 1934, I thought the schools would start closing, but there was actually a spike of new openings in 1935. That spike and broader historical context were what I wanted to look closer at for this final.

My research question for this final project is: how did the federal Indian boarding school system evolve across more than 150 years of U.S. policy? Counting new openings in Milestone 3 only told me when schools were founded, not when they were running. So for this final, I built a chart showing how many schools were operating in each year from 1819 through 2024 to get a fuller picture of the boarding schools and their (semi) full history. I also paired the chart with a long historical writeup/essay that walks through the major federal policies that might play a part in the rise and fall of the line, from the Civilization Fund Act of 1819 through the Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978.

To make the graph, I went back to the date columns I had parsed in Milestone 3 and added one more step so the code saw the dates as integers and not a string. Some schools had close years marked as "Open," meaning they were still in operation, so I flagged those and treated them as active through 2024 based on when the information in the dataset was last updated. Then I looped through each year from 1819 to 2024 and counted how many schools had open_year ≤ y and close_year ≥ y. And plotting these counts as a line chart let me overlay the major federal policy dates and see how the chart's shape lined up with each one.

The chart and research confirmed some patterns I expected and also revealed some information I did not. Schools accumulated slowly through the early 1800s, surged after the Peace Policy and Dawes Act, peaked at 238 schools in 1904, and then declined slowly across the 20th century. I discuss this in my write up, but this is what I anticipated even before milestone 3. And the 1935 spike I had been so surprised by turned out to be smaller than I thought (it was only an increase from 161 to 179 active schools), but with further research I saw why the original spike in my MVP occurred. Most of those new schools were Collier's federally-funded community day schools going up on the Navajo Reservation, while older boarding schools elsewhere stayed open because Native families needed them during the Depression. So it makes sense that a cluster of new schools with a completely different philosophy would open at once, right after the policy that was trying to implement them, rather than spread out across years like the older boarding school system did. The tailing at the end was surprising to me because I did not think that these schools would be counted as still in operation. It was then more clear to me that just because the data technically shows that these types of schools are active doesn’t mean that the schools are under the same authority and functioning the same way. This is one of the limitations to this graph. It is a little misleading. I addressed this issue in the write up. 

Other limitations of this analysis include how I had to treat "Open" as the year 2024 in my data. That means schools without explicit close years all extend to the right edge of the chart, which shapes how the flat tail looks. Additionally, there are also gaps in what the data can show. I could not find accessible enrollment information that would lend a simple dataset so there is no enrollment information, meaning my chart counts schools but not children. A small day school and a large off-reservation boarding school like Carlisle show up on the chart representing the same thing, even though their impacts on the children were very different. Lastly, there are a few computational choices I made worth acknowledging. A small number of schools in the dataset had multiple operation periods because they closed and reopened. While cleaning the code in milestone 3, I only kept the first range, so the chart treats those schools as one continuous run rather than showing the gap and second period. Thus, because I used the initial steps and data from that milestone, the final product is also affected. This affects only 17 schools out of 411, so the impact on the chart is minor, but it is still a methodological choice that smooths over some of the complexity in the data.
